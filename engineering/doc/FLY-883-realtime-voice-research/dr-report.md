# Realtime Voice-to-Voice Backend Selection for a Discord-Based AI Agent System

## Executive summary

**Recommendation:** make **Google Gemini Live API** your **default hosted backend**, specifically the **Live / native-audio line** (`gemini-live-2.5-flash-native-audio` in Google Cloud docs; `gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-preview-12-2025` on the Gemini Developer API pricing page, with `gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview` as Google’s newer low-latency Live preview pricing reference). It is the best fit for your stated weighting because Google is the strongest on **explicit multilingual / code-switching positioning**, has the **clearest production-grade async tool-calling model** for “voice front-end + external brain,” and is dramatically cheaper than OpenAI at your usage level. The main tradeoff is operational friction: Live connections are short-lived and require session-resumption/compression logic to feel “always on.” citeturn11view2turn12view0turn13view0turn10view0turn10view2turn11view1

**OpenAI Realtime** is the **operationally safest fallback**. It is generally available, has a longer documented session duration, excellent interruption semantics, direct WebRTC/WebSocket/SIP support, simultaneous audio+text output, and strong JS documentation. If you decide that session durability and implementation simplicity matter more than cost, OpenAI is the safer default. citeturn15view0turn7view0turn39search5turn17view0

A **local CosyVoice-based stack** is the best **strategic backup path** if Chinese-English code-switching becomes the decisive blocker for hosted S2S models, or if you later need stronger privacy and custom voices. But it should **not** be your default now. It is not one backend; it is a **pipeline program**: Discord audio → VAD → ASR (preferably SenseVoice / FunASR) → your Claude agent → TTS (CosyVoice 3, or CosyVoice 2 if you need mature streaming docs). The individual components are promising, especially for Chinese/Cantonese recognition and cross-lingual voice cloning, but the end-to-end voice-agent behavior, Node integration, and ops burden are materially higher. citeturn35view2turn35view1turn35view0turn33search2turn33search9turn33search0

For **your exact architecture**, the best pattern is **hybrid**: let the realtime speech model handle hearing, speaking, interruption, and lightweight turn management, while your own Claude-based agent is exposed as a **tool** for substantive reasoning and actions. Google’s async function-calling model maps especially well to this because it explicitly supports continuing the conversation while tools run and lets you schedule tool results as `SILENT`, `WHEN_IDLE`, or `INTERRUPT`. OpenAI also supports async tool calling natively in `gpt-realtime`, but Google’s documentation is more explicit and operationally helpful. citeturn13view0turn15view0turn7view0

On **Discord integration**, the real risk is not the AI vendor but the transport layer: `@discordjs/voice` still warns that **audio receive is not documented by Discord**, and Discord’s 2026 DAVE end-to-end encryption rollout means third-party voice clients and bots must support DAVE. That is workable in practice, but it is the piece most likely to create brittle ops work. citeturn27search0turn26view1turn26view2turn28search3turn28search6

## Per-backend details

### OpenAI Realtime

OpenAI’s Realtime line is the most mature “single vendor voice-agent transport” of the three. The API supports **WebRTC**, **WebSockets**, and **SIP**, exposes explicit session state and fine-grained events, supports **60-minute** sessions, and has the cleanest documented interruption flow I reviewed: if VAD is enabled and user speech begins, the server cancels the in-progress response, emits cancellation events, and expects the client to stop local playback immediately and truncate the unplayed assistant audio from conversation state. That is exactly what you want for barge-in in a Discord voice channel. citeturn7view0turn15view0

For your “external brain” design, OpenAI is viable but slightly less elegant than Google in the docs. Realtime supports tools at the session/response level, function-result injection back into the conversation, and simultaneous **audio + text** output, which is excellent for records. OpenAI’s 2025 and 2026 product announcements also say async function calling is now native in `gpt-realtime` / `GPT‑Realtime‑2`, including preambles and audible transparency like “checking that now.” That said, the low-level operational guidance around long-running tools is less specific than Google’s scheduling model. citeturn7view0turn15view0turn16view0

OpenAI’s code-switching story is promising but still somewhat under-documented for your exact bilingual pattern. OpenAI specifically says `gpt-realtime` can **switch languages mid-sentence** and that newer realtime models better handle specialized terminology and Chinese alphanumeric sequences. Those are directly relevant signals for Chinese frames with embedded English technical terms. But I did **not** find a public Mandarin-English code-switch benchmark from OpenAI comparable to SEAME / ASCEND-style reporting, so this remains a place where you should expect to run your own eval set before committing. citeturn15view0turn16view0

On cost, OpenAI is the expensive choice. Official pricing plus official audio-token accounting imply a realistic **~$43/month** for your baseline usage under a 50/50 talk split, and roughly **~$86/month** if both directions are near-continuously active every clock minute. That is still cheap in absolute terms for a single founder, but it is materially more expensive than Gemini. citeturn39search5turn39search16

### Google Gemini Live

Gemini Live is the best fit for your **hybrid voice shell** pattern. Google’s docs are unusually explicit that Live function calls are **non-blocking by default**, that slow tools should be offloaded without blocking the stream, and that tool responses can be scheduled as **`SILENT`**, **`WHEN_IDLE`**, or **`INTERRUPT`**. For a Discord bot whose “real brain” is a Claude-based company system, that means the speech model can keep a natural conversational surface while your own agent runs the substantive action. That is an unusually strong product fit for your architecture. citeturn13view0

Gemini also has the strongest official multilingual language in the sources I reviewed. The Live capabilities guide says native audio models can **switch between languages naturally during conversation**, and Google Cloud docs describe **seamless multilingual support** without preconfiguration. That is directly aligned with mixed Chinese + English technical terms. I did **not** find a primary-source Mandarin-English benchmark from Google, so this is still partly a bet on product direction and multilingual model design rather than hard zh-en leaderboard evidence. Still, among hosted APIs, Google’s position is the most favorable for your code-switching requirement. citeturn12view0turn12view2

Gemini’s biggest weakness is operational, not model quality. The connection lifetime is about **10 minutes**, audio-only sessions are **15 minutes** unless you enable context-window compression, and session resumption requires storing resumable state. Google provides a clear answer to this — resumption plus compression — but that does mean more engineering than OpenAI’s 60-minute session model. If you want “join Discord voice and just stay there for a long walk,” Gemini needs a stronger reconnect story in your bridge layer. citeturn11view1turn11view0

The economics are excellent. On Google’s current pricing, your baseline usage is roughly **~$10/month** under a 50/50 speaking split, with a generous upper bound near **~$21/month**. That is cheap enough that you can afford to keep Gemini as default and fail over to OpenAI for selected sessions without worrying much about spend. citeturn10view2turn10view0

### Local stack around CosyVoice

The local option should be thought of as an **architecture family**, not a single backend. The cleanest version for your use case is:

**Discord 48 kHz Opus** → decode to PCM → **VAD / turn segmentation** → **ASR** (prefer **SenseVoice** through FunASR / sherpa-onnx; Whisper-family is the broader fallback) → your **Claude-based agent** → **TTS** (prefer **CosyVoice 3**, fallback **CosyVoice 2** if you care most about streaming-paper maturity) → Opus encode back to Discord. citeturn33search2turn33search9turn35view2turn35view1turn35view0

For your weighted criteria, the local stack’s attraction is obvious. SenseVoice is explicitly optimized for low-latency multilingual speech understanding and is described by FunAudioLLM as having **significant advantages in Chinese and Cantonese recognition** over Whisper-style baselines, while CosyVoice 3 improves cross-lingual zh↔en synthesis quality and is built for expressive multilingual voice generation. If you later want a distinct persona voice for each “department lead” agent, the local stack is the easiest path to that without depending on a hosted vendor’s changing voice catalog or restrictions around cloning. citeturn35view2turn37view2turn35view1

The reason it should not be your default now is that the integration work is real. FunASR is easy to stand up and explicitly supports API-server deployment across macOS/Linux/Windows with CPU-only as an option, and SenseVoice runs on macOS arm64 via sherpa-onnx. But CosyVoice on Apple Silicon is still less clearly documented than the ASR side, and the highest-confidence operational path is still a Linux/CUDA environment. For a single-user founder workflow on an M-series Mac with 64–128 GB unified memory, it is **feasible**; I just would not call it the fastest-to-value route for a production Discord bridge. citeturn33search2turn33search9turn33search0turn33search4

## Architecture choice

For a Discord-based “founder talks to agent leads” system, the decisive question is not whether speech-to-speech is “cooler.” It is whether the speech layer can stay **interruptible and natural** while still letting your **own agent runtime** remain the system of record for memory, actions, and audit. On that criterion, the best pattern is **hybrid**, not pure end-to-end speech intelligence. citeturn13view0turn15view0

A **pure native speech-to-speech** design is the lowest-latency option when the conversation is simple and the speech model can answer directly. It is also the most natural for prosody, backchannels, and interruption because one model owns the whole turn loop. The downside is control: your company logic, memory, approvals, and structured actions become secondary or indirect. That is not a good fit for your system because you explicitly want voice to trigger high-value actions inside your own app. citeturn7view0turn11view2

A **modular STT → LLM → TTS** pipeline is the most controllable design. You get deterministic transcripts, full observability, easy replacement of components, and strong privacy options. It is also the cleanest way to preserve your own Claude agent as the only decision-maker. But it tends to lose conversational polish unless you stream aggressively at every stage, add filler logic, implement interruption carefully, and invest in prosody management. That is why it is a good fallback architecture and a good local/privacy architecture, but not the best default when the founder wants natural, hands-free conversation while walking or cooking. citeturn33search2turn35view0turn35view2

The **hybrid pattern** is the sweet spot: a realtime speech model handles hearing, speaking, VAD, turn-taking, and lightweight repair behavior, while substantive work is delegated via tools to your external Claude session. This is exactly what both OpenAI and Google are now optimizing for. OpenAI emphasizes preambles, parallel tool calls, and stronger recovery behavior in `GPT‑Realtime‑2`; Google goes even further and documents fully non-blocking tools plus result scheduling policies. That means the voice model can say “checking with operations” or “one moment” while your actual agent brain does the work. citeturn16view0turn13view0

For **your** case, the hybrid pattern has four concrete advantages. First, it preserves your existing Claude session model and memory as the real source of truth. Second, it lets the speech model do what it is uniquely good at — fast turn management and natural speech. Third, it gives you records, because transcripts and tool actions remain first-class artifacts. Fourth, it localizes vendor risk: if a hosted speech backend changes pricing or degrades on code-switching, you can swap only the voice shell without re-platforming your company system. citeturn7view0turn13view0turn16view0

## Discord integration in Node.js

The good news is that this is absolutely buildable today in Node/TypeScript. The bad news is that the sharp edges are mostly on the **Discord voice transport**, not the AI backend. `@discordjs/voice` still advertises audio receive with a footnote: **audio receive is not documented by Discord so stable support is not guaranteed**. In practice, people do use it, and the voice ecosystem around Discord has years of production experience, but you should treat receive as “works, but deserves its own reliability budget.” citeturn27search0turn27search8

The biggest 2026-specific issue is **DAVE**. Discord now requires DAVE end-to-end encryption support for third-party voice participants, and the company’s change log says that after the March 2026 transition, clients/applications not updated for DAVE can no longer participate in calls. The current `@discordjs/voice` docs also say that `@snazzah/davey` is the supported DAVE library and that it now comes pre-installed. That is the baseline you should assume. citeturn26view1turn26view2turn28search3turn28search4

The practical audio path is straightforward conceptually:

1. Receive **Discord Opus @ 48 kHz** from the voice channel.
2. Decode and **downmix/resample** to whatever your speech backend prefers.
3. Run a **local VAD / speaking gate** before shipping audio upstream.
4. Receive backend audio output, usually **PCM**.
5. Resample/reframe, encode back to **Opus**, and play to Discord.

Gemini is especially explicit here: it wants **16-bit PCM input**, natively **16 kHz** but will resample, and outputs **24 kHz PCM**. Existing Node Discord bridges for Gemini explicitly perform **Discord Opus receive → PCM decode → Gemini streaming**, and then **Gemini PCM → Opus encode → Discord playback**, with low-latency VAD and local barge-in gating. OpenAI’s Realtime docs are lower-level on the transport but similarly assume that your app is responsible for media plumbing when not using a browser-to-WebRTC flow. citeturn12view3turn12view0turn26view4turn7view0

There are already useful open-source pointers. For OpenAI, `Emmanuek5/openai-realtime-discordbot` is a small TypeScript/Bun Discord bot that joins voice channels, converts model audio for Discord playback, and advertises function calling support; it is not a large, battle-tested project, but it is a useful bridge example. For Gemini, `hihumanzone/Gemini-Live-discord` is directly relevant: a Node bot with multi-user mixing, Discord Opus ↔ Gemini PCM conversion, low-latency VAD, local interruption gating, and bounded reconnect behavior. For transcript-first workflows, `dtinth/discord-transcriber` shows a Node Discord bot doing voice capture, VAD, and **48 kHz stereo → 16 kHz mono PCM** preprocessing before sending audio to Gemini. citeturn26view3turn26view4turn26view5

On policy and privacy, Discord’s current Developer Terms require you to maintain a **clear privacy policy** that accurately describes what data you collect, how you use/share it, how users can request deletion, and to delete API data when it is no longer necessary or when users request deletion. Discord also requires compliance with applicable laws and regulations and reasonable security controls. I did **not** find a current Discord developer policy line that specifically bans voice recording by bots, but for anything that captures or stores voice, the safe reading is: disclose it clearly, provide deletion, minimize retention, and obtain consent as required by local law. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn32view2

## Recommendation, pluggable interface, and risks

The **default backend** I would ship first is **Gemini Live**, with **OpenAI Realtime** implemented immediately as the first alternate backend. That recommendation is grounded in your weights, not in absolute product maturity. Gemini gets the nod because the official evidence is strongest on **multilingual live conversation** and **async tool orchestration**, and the pricing is low enough that you can keep it always available. OpenAI is the fallback because it is easier to trust operationally and has cleaner session semantics for long-running synchronous experiences. The local CosyVoice stack should stay on your roadmap as the **privacy / persona / Chinese-optimization escape hatch**, not as the day-one default. citeturn12view0turn13view0turn10view2turn7view0turn15view0turn35view2

Your pluggable interface should abstract the parts that are genuinely backend-specific. At minimum, define interfaces for **audio format negotiation**; **streaming input/output frames**; **turn / VAD events** like speech-started, speech-stopped, response-started, response-cancelled; **interruption semantics** including explicit truncate/clear behavior; **tool call events** and **tool result injection**; **transcript events** for partial/final user and assistant text; **voice/persona selection**; and **session lifecycle** including reconnect/resume limits. If you do that cleanly, OpenAI, Gemini, and a local pipeline can all share the same Discord adapter and agent-runtime tool surface. citeturn7view0turn13view0turn11view1turn12view0

The most realistic risks for the recommended Gemini default are these. First, **session-management complexity**: ~10-minute connections and 15-minute audio sessions mean you must engineer resumption and buffering carefully. Second, **preview churn**: naming and pricing across Gemini Live SKUs already show some surface inconsistency between Cloud docs and Gemini API pricing pages. Third, **unverified zh-en code-switch edge cases**: Google’s official multilingual claims are good, but I did not find a public Mandarin-English benchmark to settle the question definitively. Fourth, **Discord receive fragility**: if DAVE or voice-receive regressions land in `@discordjs/voice`, your AI model choice will not save you. citeturn11view1turn11view0turn10view0turn10view2turn27search0turn28search6

If you instead choose OpenAI as default, the risk profile shifts. You reduce session-management risk and gain simultaneous audio+text output with very solid interruption semantics, but you accept materially higher cost and a less explicitly documented async tool-result scheduling model than Google’s. You also still do not have a public Mandarin-English benchmark that would let me say, with confidence, that OpenAI beats Gemini on your specific code-switching pattern. citeturn7view0turn15view0turn39search5turn16view0

## Open questions and limitations

There are three meaningful things I could **not verify to the standard I would prefer**.

I did **not** find a strong, public **Mandarin-English code-switch benchmark** for **OpenAI Realtime** or **Gemini Live** comparable to SEAME / ASCEND-style reporting. The best Chinese-centered evidence in the sources I found sits on the **local FunAudioLLM stack**, not the hosted S2S APIs. That means your final selection should include a short in-house eval set built from your founder’s actual speaking style. citeturn35view2turn37view2turn12view0turn15view0

I did **not** find a current official vendor page that publishes a clean, apples-to-apples **first-audio latency benchmark** under “realistic network conditions” for both OpenAI and Google. The OpenAI figure I used is a credible community measurement; Google’s sources are more about chunking guidance and qualitative latency claims than a matched P50/P90 number. citeturn38search6turn38search1turn38search5

I also did **not** verify a first-party **custom voice cloning** capability for either OpenAI Realtime or Gemini Live. OpenAI’s docs clearly expose built-in preset voices, while the local CosyVoice stack is the clearest path to true multi-agent custom voices. citeturn7view0turn15view0turn35view2
